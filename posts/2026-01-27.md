### Thoughts on Knowledge Inflation

As more and more research is churned out, the literal amount of knowledge available has drastically increased. As a result, it is becoming (in fact it already is) impossible to become a specialist in many different fields, at least within a reasonable timeframe. For most people wishing to develop new knowledge, they must become increasingly specialised. This is especially evident in mathematics. It is almost impossible to be a modern day Euler and touch upon a vast number of fields (applied mathematics, graph theory, number theory etc) and impact all of them in some way. 

One might worry eventually the knowledge well will run dry, and it will become impossible to make meaningful contributions at all as dissertation's become so niche that no one but the author understands it. While unlikely in the near future, it is definitely a reasonable concern.

What I believe we're now beginning to experience is **learning inflation**. In the past, learning things took a lot of time. You had to read source material, watch videos, and arrange consultation sessions with your professors. However, with the not-so-recent advent of LLMs, it has become far faster to learn by simply talking to chat. I'm not saying that every conceivable subject can be handled in such a way, but the reality is there are a very great number that can (especially programming).

I must state that this learning style is not so naive as asking "please teach me about neural networks" but rather takes a more top-down approach. Consider the following dialogue between user and LLM:

"I'm learning about neural networks, and I hear something about matrix multiplication. What's that? Give me an example to practice."  
"Matrix multiplication is yadda dadda wubba lubba"  
"Okay I tried your example but my number in the top right is 5. Why is this result 5? If I multiply 2x2 it's 4 not 5."  
"That's because..."  
"Okay, so correct me if I'm wrong, but based on my understanding, to apply matrix multiplication in neural networks we want to multiply this by..."  
"That's almost correct! You daba dodo lala shushu"  

What we can see here is that we begin with a *need*. We need to learn about neural networks. Then we recursively break down the requirements of that need by asking about prerequisite X, and maybe we also ask about X's prerequesite Y. We keep doing this until we can confidently declare that we completely understand a certain topic. To wrap up our learning, we try to explain the topic to the LLM and ask it to correct us if we get anything wrong. In between, we mix in certain phrases such as "explain this to me like I'm 12" or "give me an example to try". 

As it turns out, this is an almost disgustingly effective way to learn extremely high-level concepts at an alarming speed. Previously, to attempt to do something like this, you'd need to pay a private tutor a lot of money to sit by your side and patiently explain things. And the truth is sometimes there are things they can't explain either. You might come from a physics background and want to understand it using some physics analogy, but the tutor doesn't know much about that so he can't. Well, the LLM can. It can do that and more. While we should be cautious of hallucinations and what not, the reality is most modern state-of-the-art LLMs really don't hallucinate much at all. If they are unsure, they are usually very capable of calling tools and searching the internet for help.

I believe we will enter an interesting era where as LLMs advance and become better at explaining things using tools (in fact, Claude can already generate interactive visualisations to explain the concept at hand), learning will simply continue to accelerate. While this will definitely create disparities between those who know how to utilise the LLM and those who don't, the grace here is that learning has become incredibly accessible. Knowledge is no longer kept behind the gates of expensive universities but rather your internet provider. Theoretically, this should result in a much more meritrocratic competition for desirable careers. Maybe one day AGI will come and none of that will matter because we'll simply get outpaced by AI learning from AI. But until then, I'm really excited to learn really quickly.
