How hard do we try to predict the future? Listening to a recent podcast with Jensen Huang made me realise the answer was probably not enough. One pertinent section is when he is asked to share his wisdom on being a visionary. He replied that he spends long periods thinking about what will happen in a particular domain. He chains thoughts together until he comes to an conclusion. He describes his conclusions as absolute. There is no doubt in his mind that any other outcome is possible. 

People who trade make similar predictions. They perform laborious quantitative and qualitative research before suggesting an action such as buying or selling a stock. Research isn't limited to Googling either; it often involves generating creative ways to get asymmetric information. I was reminded of this when listening to an equity researcher from UBS share about his methodology for evaluating certain stocks in retail. Naturally, he'd start from the basics such as searching up recent earnings or relevant news, but he wouldn't halt there. He'd also have teams go into stores and check the stock levels of different items to determine general demand, talk to employees to gauge sentiment, and also scan satellite images of carparks to determine shopper volume. The point is, people who trade do a lot of research. 

This prompted me to think back to some of the major decisions I've made in my life - the college I went to, the major I studied,the friends I chose etc. Did I even think about these half as hard? Did I really try and make a prediction on how the future would pan out in the next 4-5 years, and the expected value of computer science then, or did I more decide solely based on whether I liked studying it and the prestige associated with it? (it was the latter)

I would still say I lucked out because I do enjoy programming and it's something that still holds relevance today. The college I chose wasn't so lucky though. I enjoy living and working in the US. Choosing a college in Singapore has made this much more difficult. I suppose then you'd ask: "Well is it reallyyyy possible to know that? I mean how can anyone know what they'll like sometime down the line?" I think there are two main hurdles to this.

One issue is that there are only so few major decisions in someone's life. Situations with short feedback loops don't require large degrees of thinking because we can simply learn from the outcome. Consider a student making a mistake when practicing a song on the piano. The feedback (the wrong note) is immediate and they can simply try again. In contrast, the repercussions of major life decisions often only materialise after months or years. It's hard to learn from a wrong choice when you've only got a few chances.  

The other is that people are rather fickle beings. It's generally true that our interests evolve over time as we meet new people and try new things. I think it's almost impossible to predict exactly who you'll meet or what you'll try. You can get a vague idea - if I do an MBA I'll probably meet lots of working adults, some with business experience - but their exact profiles are almost impossible to pinpoint. 

With those hurdles in mind, I don't believe we should expect ourselves to really be able to predict the future. There are a lot more people with wrong grandiose visions than right. But I believe there is some principal in trying. I believe there is great value in going out of your way to gather intel on important decisions in your life. I could have easily chosen to make a trip to the US for at least a week before I chose my college, but I never did that. I would encourage you (my dear reader) to really probe and dissect important choices you make. You don't even have to wait for an important choice - you can practice on slightly less important ones. What club you join, who you talk to at an event, or how you will spend your next weekends are all good experiments. Observe the consequences of these choices and align them with your initial predictions. Did joining the band make you as happy as you thought it would? Maybe two months down the line you realise no. You accounted for the fact you'd like being in a band because you've tried playing certain instruments, but you failed to account for how all band people don't shower.

Even thinking back now, there were some obvious signs I'd probably enjoy being in the states more than Singapore. It was clear from my interactions with students from international schools that I mesh better with people who weren't from here. Obviously I'm not saying there's anything wrong with Singaporeans. I just happen to maybe enjoy talking about different things from them!

Ultimately, even if I studied overseas, I probably wouldn't have chosen the exact college that would bring me the most joy. Maybe I'd end up in Europe or something. I don't believe that by attempting to predict the future, we will arrive at the optimal outcome. I do think however that it eliminates a space of poor outcomes, and we can expect to enjoy a much better one.
