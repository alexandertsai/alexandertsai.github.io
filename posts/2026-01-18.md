### Small things

Today while at Costco I bought a small hot dog and threw out the receipt. I didn't know this yet but you aren't supposed to do that.. When my mom's friend (who was bringing us around SF) later asked where the receipt was, I reported I had thrown it away gulp. She told me at Costco they check your receipt when leaving the restaurant. Realising my mistake, I headed back to the dustbin and attempted to fish it out. Forgive me for being human but this task was kinda nasty. The receipt was visible yet slightly too deep into the dustbin to comfortably fish out. I stood there looking kinda lost at the dustbin for a second. Just then, a stranger approached me and asked: "lose something?" I told him I had lost my receipt. He then demonstrated how to open the dustbin such that I could easily fish it out. I thanked him and got the receipt and headed back to join my mom and her friend.

A few days ago I was passing through customs in Japan. The lady working at the airport started by asking me if I was travelling alone (hitori desu-ga?), to which I replied "hai, hitori-desu". She then flipped through a booklet showing various illegal items, pointing to each item and asking me in Japanese whether I had that item with me. I repeatedly went "arimasen" (meaning "no" in Japanese). Following which, we have a couple of micro-interactions here and there. While I am packing up my luggage to go, she makes the comment that my Japanese is good, and asks if I had studied Japanese. I was a bit surprised by that question and my immediate thought was that there's no way I can tell her my Japanese is built on Google and Anime. I ended up blurting out that my mom spoke Japanese and I had learned it from her (a complete lie). ANYWAY I thought this was a really sweet interaction.

The last interaction I want to talk about is when I was skiing. If I remember correctly it was my second day in Japan. The day before had been full of many falls and running into plants and trees so I was feeling slightly demoralised. I was also very tired because falling on your skis is EXHAUSTING. The issue is that every time you fall down your skis pop off (especially when the fall involves tumbling of some sort) and so you're forced to put them back on by retrieving the four pieces of your ski set (your two poles and two skis, usually scattered somehow to demonically maximise retrieval effort), and then you've gotta dig your skis into the snow to find a way that you can stand on stable ground and put your skis on. As I was thinking about my fatigue from the day before, I spotted this guy that seemed to be stuck in the exact situation I had just described. Thinking about how bad it felt to be in that position, I swooped down and helped him out! I held his skis for him while he clicked back in. I made the usual small talk about how this slope was really slippery and that I had fallen earlier too then left.

When I think about these moments it's pretty clear they're nowhere near as monumental as the ones you experience when you graduate or get married. Nevertheless, I remember them vividly. I believe there's a lot of understated value in small moments like this. You could argue that the joy/satisfaction you feel in life is just as much the sum of the many small things as the large singular moments. I felt really happy when I got the internship I wanted, and I felt incredibly joyful when I got good grades, and I'm still reminded of those good things every now and then. But I think if joy was only derived from those big moments in life most people would be miserable for 90% of the time. A lot of the intermediate tiny tiny things, like seeing someone you know on your journey home or side-eyeing the person to your right when you spot someone tweaking out at Target are incredibly valuable too.

I like to measure the theoretical importance of certain events based on their likelihood of occurring to you on your deathbed. I know it's a bit morbid but I really don't know of any objective methods to do this. I don't believe any of these moments will occur to me then (hopefully not anytime soon!) but I think the cumulative joy derived will (consciously or subconsciously) make it a lot easier for someone to say they lived a satisfactory life.
