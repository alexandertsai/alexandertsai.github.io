On my flight to SF on Friday I noticed the person next to me was doing homework. His manner of doing so was mostly taking screenshots of his assignment, pasting it into ChatGPT, and then copying that answer back.

I honestly don't think there's an issue with students using genAI in their schoolwork. It reflects the lived reality of working life today - a large number of my colleagues are more focused on getting their AI to do their work for them than the work itself.

However, I do think there is an issue with the classes failing to reflect this reality. There are still an outrageous number of classes that continuously try and ban AI or refuse to acknowledge it's existence.

While the need for understanding the fundamentals still remains (e.g. reading and writing simple statements such as for loops and while loops), I believe the approach of handing students a task such as writing the code to generate a star using the Turtle library is archaic. Instead, we should teach only what is necessary. Students need only to be able to write and understand simple code. The rest just isn't necessary. Thus, it simply does not make sense to hand students graded practices that involve such manual coding when we know most of them are just going to pass it into Chat anyway.

One way to resolve this is to have students take in-person exams on a lockdown browser that only permits access to an offline code editor and weight exams heavily. Another way would be to simply make tasks much more difficult - difficult to the extent wherein they need to really understand what they are doing to make AI work in the first place. For foundational courses, the latter is unrealistic.

It should be understood that there is a nuance here. I'm not saying we should stick to traditional ways of teaching, but rather drastically shorten the timelines to only teach students what is necessary. Previously, a student may need to know how to write at least 20-30 lines of code and understand data structures and algorithms. Today, we can alter these introductory courses to focus less on memorising syntax and more on broadly understand the programming process. Instead of spending 12 lessons on Python syntax and solving difficult problems, spend 2 lessons on syntax, quiz them on reading code, another 2 lessons on packages and virtual environments, quiz them on that, and then another lesson on navigating the terminal and running curl commands etc. 

My point is that the knowledge and skills needed to complete a task well has drastically decreased. No one writes code anymore so we don't really need to know how to write it - just enough so that students don't stare at a script the same way we stare at Greek text. I believe that the appropriate change here is for institutions to drastically widen the scope of each course, covering a far greater amount of content at a much shallow level. The reality is that we just don't need to understand things as deeply since the inception of The Great Abstractor (AI).

If you asked me what are the important skills in this post-AI world I'd argue that understanding is the new bottleneck. Anyone can ask AI to build something useful, but it is becoming increasingly rare that people are able to prompt and produce something great. And there are layers to this. First you need to know how to use AI. How do I ask a prompt that gives it the right context and provide an appropriate environment for it to carry out its task? Then, how do I manage that context and ensure it's aligned? Once I know how to use it, how can I use my brain to provide it with strong guidance to do this task and produce something amazing? As much as the labs hate to admit it, there are simply things AI is not that great at yet. Humans still have value. 

The other day I had a conversation with James about his experience at YC startup school. He told me that he was confused by conflicting pieces of advice by Boris Cherny (developer of Claude Code) and Jeff Dean (Chief Scientist at Google Deepmind). Boris argues you should be less specific with Claude while Jeff proclaims you should be as specific as possible. This split really reveals two scenarios. One is where you're dumber than the AI. If you know nothing about software engineering, you should allow Claude to make more decisions for you. This would work better than if you gave Claude suboptimal instructions and caused it to build a worst product than if it were to receive no guidance at all. However, if you're smarter than the AI (at least in the specific domain you're working on), you should be more specific. It's highly likely you have far more context (possibly such a vast amount that it would be difficult to communicate it all) about the task at hand and understand what the best solution is. You might understand the project will probably end up scaling like such, so we need this, but we also should weigh our managers opinion heavily, so he'll probably like this, but also we know this new technology is coming out so we should consider that etc etc. AI will give you generic answers that generally work, but lose their edge.

I still stand by the value of people in this AI age. It is simply the output quantity and quality expected of you is much more. Learning has accelerated and we are expected to have greater breadths of knowledge to continue to be useful in our work. Keep learning and understanding! Use AI to teach not just to do. We still have value.
